What the research says about screen light

We read the studies on screen colour and brightness by time of day. Here's what they show, what they don't, and how Awning's Day, Evening and Night settings follow from them.

  • What matters most is how much blue-green light reaches your eyes. Researchers call it melanopic light. In the evening, more of it means melatonin, the hormone that rises before sleep, comes later and lower (Brown 2022, Schöllhorn 2023).
  • Warmth and brightness work together. Warming a screen on its own removes only about half of that light. Dimming it too removes far more (Brown 2022, Nagare 2019).
  • During the day, there's no reason to warm your screen. Daytime light should be bright, and it mostly comes from daylight and room lights, not your screen (Brown 2022).
  • Red light at night has the least of it. In lab studies, red light didn't lower melatonin. It still isn't completely without effect (Figueiro 2009, Ho Mien 2014).
  • Some popular claims don't hold up. Studies have not shown that warm screens improve sleep in everyday life, or that filtering blue light reduces eye strain (Duraccio 2021, Cochrane 2023).

Why light in the evening matters

Besides the cells you see with, your eyes have cells that tell your body clock whether it's day. They're most sensitive to blue-green light, around 480 nanometres (Brainard 2001, Lockley 2003). Scientists measure the light that reaches them as melanopic light, in lux, using an international standard (CIE S 026).

In the evening, it takes surprisingly little. In one study, ordinary room light before bed delayed melatonin in almost everyone (Gooley 2011). In another, the most sensitive people reacted strongly to just 10 lux, about the light of a dim lamp. People's sensitivity varied more than fifty-fold (Phillips 2019).

Screens count. Reading on a bright tablet for four hours before bed lowered melatonin by about half and pushed it about an hour and a half later, compared with reading a printed book (Chang 2015). In a study of 72 people, the effect on melatonin and on how long people took to fall asleep followed the amount of melanopic light from the screen, not how bright it looked (Schöllhorn 2023).

In 2022, eighteen experts in the field published recommendations for healthy adults (Brown 2022).

WhenRecommended melanopic light at the eye
Daytimeat least 250 lux, ideally from daylight
Evening, from 3 hours before bedat most 10 lux
While sleepingas dark as possible, at most 1 lux

These targets are for all the light reaching your eyes, from the room as well as the screen. Awning can't measure that, and it doesn't claim to meet them. They're meant for adults aged 18 to 55 with ordinary daytime schedules, not shift workers, children or older people.

Warmth alone isn't enough

A screen sends out a mix of light. Warming it takes out blue and some green. Dimming it takes out a share of everything. The two multiply, so it helps to do both.

Warming on its own does less than people expect. The 2022 recommendations put the gain from changing a screen's colour alone at about half (Brown 2022). On an iPad at full brightness, Night Shift's warmest setting didn't lower melatonin significantly more than its coolest one, and the researchers suggested dimming the screen as well (Nagare 2019). In a week-long trial with 167 young adults, Night Shift made no measurable difference to sleep (Duraccio 2021).

Bigger changes do show up. Light from the red end of the spectrum disturbed sleep and melatonin less than blue light (Green 2017). Researchers measuring tablets and phones recommended a bedtime mode that both shifts colour toward red and lowers the backlight (Gringras 2015). That's what Awning's evening and night presets do.

How Day, Evening and Night follow from this

Each is a starting point. Pick one in the app, move the sliders, and click Save to make it yours. Why each one is set where it is.

DAY · 5000K · 100%

A slight tint, if you like one.

The research gives no reason to warm your screen during the day. Bright days seem to make you less sensitive to light in the evening (Hébert 2002, Rångtell 2016), and that light comes from windows and room lights, not the screen. Most people find a display white of about 5,400–7,300K the most natural (Choi 2016, Li 2026). DAY starts just below that range, for people who prefer a slightly softer screen. Move it to whatever is comfortable, or turn Awning off during the day.

Evidence: none for warming by day
EVENING · 2700K · 55%

Warm, and noticeably dimmer.

This is the part of the day that research supports most clearly. Evening screen light lowers and delays melatonin, and the effect grows with the amount of melanopic light (Chang 2015, Schöllhorn 2023). Since warmth alone only goes about halfway, EVENING dims the screen to 55% too. Go lower if your room is dim. Experts suggest starting about three hours before bed (Brown 2022), which Awning's sunset schedule can do for you.

Evidence: strong in the lab, mixed for sleep
NIGHT · 0K · 40%

Red only, and dim.

Red light hardly reaches the body clock's sensors. In lab studies, red light didn't lower melatonin at all (Figueiro 2009, Ho Mien 2014). After a bright screen, your eyes need time to adjust to a dark room. In one study, red light didn't cause that delay, but white and blue did (Mayr 2010). Red light isn't completely without effect, though: it can make you more alert (Figueiro 2009, Sahin 2013), and in one study it shifted the body clock of some people (Ho Mien 2014). At night, darker is still better. No study has yet tested a red computer screen itself.

Evidence: moderate, from lab studies

Our rough numbers

We estimated how much melanopic light each preset leaves, using the international standard (CIE S 026) and measured colour spectra of typical LCD and CRT screens. These are calculations, not measurements of a Mac. The DAY and EVENING rows were scaled from that calculation for the October 2026 settings (5000K, and 55% brightness), not recalculated from the spectra. A real screen may differ.

SettingMelanopic light left, compared with a plain white screen
Awning off100%
DAYabout 75–85%
EVENINGabout 7–9%
NIGHTwell under 1%

A laptop showing a white page at full brightness, about half a metre away, gives roughly 100 lux or more of melanopic light. By our estimate, EVENING brings that worst case down to about 10 lux, around the level the experts suggest, and NIGHT to well under 1 lux. Ordinary pages are darker than plain white, so most of the time the real numbers are lower.

The names on the warmth slider, such as 2700K, describe the colour Awning aims for. They aren't a measured colour temperature of your screen.

What the research doesn't show

Some claims made for warm screens go further than the evidence. Awning doesn't make them.

That night modes improve sleep

Lab studies show clear effects on melatonin. Studies of sleep in everyday life are mixed, and several found no difference (Duraccio 2021, Blume 2022, Rångtell 2016, Shechter 2020).

That less blue light means less eye strain

A Cochrane review and several blinded trials found that filtering blue light didn't reduce eye strain (Cochrane 2023, Palavets 2019, Vera 2022). Eye doctors suggest matching screen brightness to the room and taking breaks (AAO).

That screens damage your eyes

The blue light from a screen measured well under 1% of the international safety limit, far less than a clear sky (O'Hagan 2016). Awning doesn't protect your eyes from harm, because screens aren't harming them.

That one setting suits everyone

Sensitivity to evening light varies more than fifty-fold between people (Phillips 2019), and comfort is personal. Treat the presets as starting points.

About flicker

Many screens dim by pulsing the backlight. Flicker from old fluorescent office lights, at 100 times a second, was linked to headaches and eye strain in a careful study (Wilkins 1989). Much faster flicker can't be seen directly, but some people notice it when their eyes move, even at several thousand pulses a second (Roberts 2013, Brown 2020).

For screens specifically, the evidence is thin. The one controlled study we found, on phones, found no difference in eye fatigue between pulsed and steady dimming (Wu 2023). Flicker-free mode is for people who know they're sensitive to flicker. It isn't a treatment for anything.
